Problem Statement and Research Questions

With an increasing number of governments legalizing marijuana, a psychedelic renaissance in full swing, and numerous experiments in decriminalization and other harm reduction programs underway, the present drug policy landscape is in flux. These developments are testing the limits of the international drug policy regime complex that has prohibited a significant number of psychoactive substances for the past century. With legalization, new problems and questions are coming into clearer focus: the deleterious environmental effects and labor conditions of industrial-scale cannabis production, for example; the mix of the market competition and technological innovations driving up THC concentrations to potentially dangerous levels in legal cannabis products; the challenge of redirecting legal weed profits to address the harm caused by racially biased prohibition in Black and Brown communities, as well as the growing impoverishment of farming families in countries with long traditions of marijuana cultivation. Adjacent to such newly legalized markets, much older problems posed by legal, prescription psychoactives persist and deepen as pharmaceutical corporations push to expand markets across unequal geographies. In recent years, for example, we have witnessed an opioid crisis across North America at the same time that access to pain medication in the post-colonial world remains grossly insufficient; the recent surge of ADHD diagnoses and prescription stimulant use appears to be following similar patterns.

At the same time, the structures and practices consolidated under prohibition show no signs of disappearing any time soon. The growth of complex, transnational criminal organizations that have profited from the black market are arguably more powerful than they have ever been. Similarly, the expanded powers and budgets that governments have granted to police forces, militaries, counternarcotic bureaucracies and prisons have left the carceral and military arms of the state empowered and entrenched in everyday life. In some countries, such developments have fed into broader political processes that have arguably reconfigured the very structure and operation of the state. Meanwhile, even where legalization has taken place, the exercise of new laws remains racialized, classed, and gendered and the stigma attached to formerly illegal drugs and their users is alive and well. As recent strongman leaders such as Bukele, Duterte, and Trump make evident, such stigma can all too easily be leveraged to win the popularity of disgruntled publics via tired ‘get-tough’ ‘war-on-drugs’ playbooks. Nor should we lose sight of the fact that in thirty-four countries, drug offences remain punishable by the death penalty.

How are we to make sense of this complex and shifting landscape? What language and narratives must we develop to articulate new directions in drug policy? What practices could help us undo the harms inflicted by the war on drugs? What new ethical questions and possibilities does the present drug policy landscape present us with? As a research network, we explore whether and how “post-prohibition” can serve as an analytic tool for opening new lines of research, pedagogy, and action more attuned and responsive to the present specificities of the global drug policy landscape. Based on a series of eight virtual discussions that took place over the course of the academic year 2024–25 and a two-day hybrid workshop that took place in June 2025, we have articulated the following, provisional analytical contours of “post-prohibition.”

“Post-Prohibition” as an analytic

Temporality

Wary of the linear progress narratives that often animate drug policy reform discourse, we do not take the “post” in “post-prohibition” to refer to a historical step forward into a new, more progressive era beyond prohibition. Rather, we see it as an analytic that encourages critical engagement with the past, present, and future of drug policy. With regard to the past, “post-prohibition” invites us to join other historians who are rethinking the standard historical narratives and concepts that have animated the drug policy reform movement to date. With the ground that has been gained vis-à-vis marijuana legalization, for example, there now seems to be more room to complicate certain overly simplified (or in some cases patently false) activist narratives that were deployed in the fight for legalization. So too have historians begun to complicate the standard story that casts prohibition as a monolithic imperial project of racialized social control and recover a more complex understanding of the myriad political projects that have lent support to anti-drug policies at different points in time. Similarly, now that the global drug policy regime has revealed itself to be more flexible and fragmented than it was once understood to be, new lines of historical inquiry might shed light on the zones of exception that activists and states have managed to create while maneuvering within the prohibitionist frameworks of global drug policy. Such histories, in turn, might hold clues for how we make sense of the present as well as imagine potential futures if and as the global consensus around prohibition continues to unravel. With regard to the present, “post-prohibition” encourages us to grapple with the continuities and inertias of prohibition that persist in the face of new legal exceptions while attending to the possible problems and futures those exceptions are opening up. With regard to the future, we refrain from attaching “post-prohibition” to a singular, normative agenda for what should come next in terms of drug policy. Instead, our hope is that, as an analytic, it can harbor a plurality of future-making projects while fostering critical deliberation about their potential payoffs and pitfalls.

Geography

Our thinking builds off existing research agendas that have begun exploring the contours of “post-prohibition” in California in relation to cannabis. That said, we recognize the necessity of expanding upon this scholarship to develop a concept of post-prohibition that is responsive to the transnational nature of drugs and drug policy. If we are to understand the lived experiences and potential future-making projects that are taking shape within the unsettled drug policy landscape of the present, we need also to engage with the realities of farmers, producers, traffickers, consumers, state agents and more-than-human actors along supply chains that stretch beyond (and, in some cases, entirely bypass) the US. Drawing from the rich body of scholarship that has explored the spatiality of economic forms, worlds, and lives, our hope is to encourage more fine-grained analysis into the geographically differentiated and unevenly distributed experiences of prohibition, legalization, regulation, and so on, with an eye to assembling a more variegated map of the past, present, and potential future of the drug policy landscape.

Epistemology

We see “post-prohibition” as a necessarily transdisciplinary analytic. The complexities of drugs and drug policy are too great to remain the purview of the medical, public health, legal or policy experts who normally claim authority over this topic. To this end, we seek to develop an analytic informed by a range of disciplinary knowledges including anthropology, critical criminology, education policy studies, history, psychology, philosophy, public health, sociology, and science and technology studies. We also think it absolutely necessary to engage knowledges that are generated beyond the academy, including that generated by artists, educators, activists and those with lived experience of drugs and prohibition at different parts along illicit supply chains.

Methodology

Given our commitments to transdisciplinarity and epistemic plurality, we are a big tent when it comes to methods and methodology. At the most general level, though, we see value in pursuing a broadly comparative research agenda:

Between different psychoactive substances: each substance has its own effects, its own set of problems, its own history, market, geography, politics. Thinking comparatively across different substances can help call such specificities into sharper relief, while also identifying possible lessons or models (pertaining to research, pedagogy and/or action) that might be transferable from one substance to another. We are particularly interested in thinking comparatively across—or rather, in blurring the very division between—legal and illegal substances. Such comparisons offer a helpful antidote against facile arguments that frame the legalization of all illegal psychoactive substances as a panacea to the ills currently associated with them. As those of us who study substances like alcohol, tobacco and pharmaceuticals can attest, however, stigma, punishment, violence, predatory practices and social suffering are still very much part and parcel of the markets that have grown up around these legal substances. Grappling with such comparative case studies can push us beyond simply calling for legalization in the abstract and help us articulate sharper, more nuanced proposals for the kinds of legal regulation we want to see.

Between psychoactive substances and other ‘addictive’ and/or prohibited phenomena: alongside public concern about illegal drugs, there is now a host of other phenomena that are problematized in terms very similar to drugs: our addiction to social media, pornography, gaming, gambling, pharmaceuticals and AI and its negative impact on our mental and cognitive health, for example; or the impact of our addiction to ultra-processed foods on our physical health. Might some of the non-carceral, non-punitive practices that have emerged or are emerging to help regulate our relationship to some of these areas contain lessons for thinking about how we could learn to relate to drugs in non-punitive, non-criminalized ways? Could the history and present realities of drug prohibition, in turn, serve as a cautionary tale for those who are currently advocating for the prohibition of smartphone use, pornography or sports gambling, for example? Might the lessons of harm reduction be of broader relevance to all of these areas? Might recent critiques of twenty-first-century sex-positive feminism hold lessons for contemporary drug-positive activism? Might thinking of the subject of the ‘user’ help us theorize the connections between these different phenomena and open new avenues for collaboration and solidarity between the scholars, educators and activists concerned with them?

Between contexts, practices and intersecting identities: how might thinking across the various geographical contexts in which we work help us recognize both their specificities as well as opportunities for solidarity? What (if any) solidarities might emerge, for example, if we look at the caste dynamics that surround alcohol in South India, the racialized dynamics of drug policing in the UK, Black and working-class youth’s experience of Brazil’s drug war as it is waged in urban schools, and experiences of gender and sexual diversity and drug use in Mexico in comparative perspective? To what extent do the practices of harm reduction and drug user activism as they have been developed, say, in Mexico, Colombia, the UK and Switzerland, hold lessons or cautionary tales for practitioners elsewhere? How might our respective understandings of drug education, harm reduction or even ‘addiction’ change if we approach such concepts from an Islamic Scientific Critical Consciousness or via different cultural scripts? What can activists and activism teach those of us following more traditional academic trajectories and vice versa?

“Post-Prohibition” as a Research Network

At our in-person workshop, we agreed on the importance of articulating a series of shared principles/ethics to guide the work of our heterogeneous, interdisciplinary network. To this end, we arrived at the following list of commitments. The members of the Post-Prohibition research network are committed to:

• Critically examining and updating the ontologies, narratives, categories, and binaries shaping drug policy discourse.
• Grappling with uncertainty while nurturing diverse future-making projects.
• Developing a comparative frame for analyzing prohibition across drugs, technology, and other domains that are problematized for the “addictive” potential.
• Building analytic tools and genealogies that trace how prohibition (and legalization) operates across time and contexts.
• Building bridges beyond academia to engage a broader range of experiences and knowledges.
• Cultivating a praxis of transformation and liberation that allows us to envision and enact alternatives to punitive prohibitions.
• Developing mechanisms to keep us accountable and responsive to non-academic partners and practitioners, and open to diverse onto-epistemological and ideological foundations, particularly decolonial, Indigenous, and non-Western frameworks.
• Producing not only traditional academic publications, but also pedagogical tools and other materials of use to activists.
